## Why this matters

Cannabis toxicosis in dogs has increased in North America and is often missed because owners deny exposure, but most dogs recover completely [1][3].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- Exposure is often denied or unknown: think cannabis in a young dog that turns acutely ataxic and hyperesthetic after being outside [1].
- Human urine drug screens can be unreliable in dogs, so a negative screen does not exclude cannabis [1][4].
- Edibles are a common source, and the differential includes xylitol and other central nervous system toxins [3][4].

## Recognition

- In 223 dogs, the commonest signs were ataxia (88%), hyperesthesia (75%), lethargy (63%), urinary incontinence (46%) and vomiting (26%). Median age was 1 year, and 28% became acutely neurologic after going outside or to a public place. 68% of owners denied a possible exposure [1].
- Vitals were often normal, but hyperthermia (23%), tachycardia (37%) and, in the 61 measured, systemic hypertension (61%) were common. Mild hyperkalemia and mild ionized hypercalcemia were common [1].
- After ingestion, clinical effects begin within about 60 minutes. Other signs described include depression, hypersalivation, mydriasis, tremors, hypothermia and bradycardia, and at higher doses agitation, tachycardia and seizures [4].
- Ingestion of human feces containing THC caused marijuana toxicosis in 15 dogs from parks and beaches [5].

## Management

- Treatment is largely supportive, with no specific antidote. Monitor temperature, heart rate and rhythm; benzodiazepine sedation may be needed for severe agitation; IV fluids help with prolonged vomiting and temperature control [4].
- In the 223-dog series, 22 dogs were hospitalized and all survived [1]. In a veterinarian survey, most pets were treated as outpatients with monitoring and most recovered completely, though 16 deaths were reported, with confounders that cannot be excluded [3].
- In 38 dogs with suspected cannabinoid intoxication, plasma THC was quantifiable in 37, and the authors concluded a clinical diagnosis from history and signs has high specificity [2].
- Human urine drug screens: false negatives can occur if testing is too soon after ingestion, and interference from canine urine metabolites is described [4]. In a 15-dog series all 8 dogs screened were positive [5].
