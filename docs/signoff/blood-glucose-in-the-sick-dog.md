## Why this matters

Both low and high blood glucose were common in non-diabetic dogs at an emergency room and both were associated with higher mortality, while low glucose as a cause of coma carried a better outlook than other causes, so a glucose check is a cheap, high-yield early test.

## Claims that differ from human practice

- In 660 non-diabetic emergency dogs, hyperglycemia (above 120 mg/dL) was found in 40.1% and hypoglycemia (below 80 mg/dL) in 9.0%; mortality was 33.2% with hyperglycemia and 44.6% with hypoglycemia against 24.9% with normal glucose.
- In dogs in coma or stupor, hypoglycemia was the cause in 10.7%, and death was less likely when hypoglycemia was the cause than for other causes.

## Dog vs human compare rows

- **Abnormal glucose in emergency patients** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Stress hyperglycemia is common in critically ill people, and both very low and very high glucose are linked to worse outcomes in people. | canine: In 660 non-diabetic dogs with glucose measured within 6 hours of arrival, hyperglycemia (above 120 mg/dL, 6.7 mmol/L) occurred in 40.1% and hypoglycemia (below 80 mg/dL, 4.4 mmol/L) in 9.0%. Hyperglycemia was most often linked to gastrointestinal disease, trauma, neoplasia and respiratory disease; hypoglycemia most often to sepsis, gastrointestinal disease and neoplasia. Dogs in shock or with raised lactate had higher glucose. Mortality was 33.2% (hyperglycemia), 44.6% (hypoglycemia) and 24.9% (normal).
- **Hypoglycemia as a cause of coma or stupor** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Hypoglycemia is a rapidly reversible cause of altered consciousness that is checked for early in people. | canine: Among 168 dogs presenting in coma or stupor to a multicenter out-of-hours service, the commonest determined causes were traumatic brain injury (16.0%), hypoglycemia (10.7%), shock (10.1%) and seizure (9.5%). Death was less likely when coma or stupor was attributed to hypoglycemia than for other etiologies; Chihuahuas were overrepresented.
- **Insulin-induced hypoglycemic seizures in diabetic dogs** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Seizures from insulin-induced hypoglycemia in people are treated by correcting glucose, and long-term antiseizure drugs are not usually needed. | canine: In 49 dogs with glucose below 80 mg/dL within 12 hours of insulin, 34 had seizures. Seizures were more likely in dogs under 10 kg or over 25 kg. Antiepileptic drugs were used in 7 of 34 dogs and 3 needed long-term therapy; insulin type, amount and time to glucose correction did not differ between seizing and non-seizing dogs.
