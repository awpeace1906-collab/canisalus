## Why this matters

Burns in dogs are uncommon, and only small canine case series were retrieved; heat-related burns can follow sun exposure in hot weather and can accompany heat exhaustion or heatstroke [1].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- A dog's coat can hide burns: in 16 dogs with sun-related dorsal burns, lesions were alopecia, erythema, ulcers, eschar and crusts on the back, mostly in dark, short-coated dogs in hot months [1].
- Signs of heat exhaustion or heatstroke preceded the skin lesions in 4 of 16 dogs, so treat a burned dog from hot weather as a possible heat illness patient too (see the heat stroke module) [1].

## Recognition

- Dorsal thermal necrosis: 15 of 16 cases occurred May to September in the southwestern USA; histology matched partial and full-thickness thermal burns [1].
- Most were treated supportively with analgesics and antimicrobials; most healed by second intention and 2 dogs had surgery [1].

## Management

- RECOVER 2026: cool thermal burns in dogs and cats with cool running tap water for at least 20 minutes, as soon as possible and within 3 hours of injury (strong, moderate quality); if running water is unavailable, a cool water-soaked towel or cold pack for at least 20 minutes (weak, moderate quality); no active cooling when risk to the rescuer is high (strong, expert opinion) [3].
- Caustic chemical exposure of the skin: irrigate immediately with running water for as long as possible, at least 15 minutes, with the rescuer in gloves, long sleeves, face and eye protection (strong, very low quality for irrigation; strong, expert opinion for protection); do not irrigate when risk to the rescuer is high [3].
