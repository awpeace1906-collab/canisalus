## Why this matters

Cardiopulmonary arrest can be survivable in dogs, and good functional outcomes have been reported even after prolonged CPR and out-of-hospital arrest [5].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- Compression technique depends on chest shape: wide-chested dogs use a modified technique [2].
- Start chest compressions without a pulse check [4].
- When not intubated, use bag-mask ventilation with a tight-fitting mask and oxygen, not mouth-to-nose [2].

## Management

- Start chest compressions without pulse palpation [4].
- Emphasis on high-quality, uninterrupted chest compressions, with a modified compression technique suggested for wide-chested dogs [2].
- Ventilation is recommended for all animals. When intubation is not possible, bag-mask ventilation with a tight-fitting facemask and oxygen is recommended rather than mouth-to-nose ventilation [1][2].
- High-dose epinephrine is no longer recommended, and atropine, if used, is given only once [1][3].
- For a shockable rhythm where the first defibrillation fails, the ALS guideline recommends doubling the defibrillator dose once and suggests vasopressin (or epinephrine if vasopressin is unavailable), esmolol, lidocaine in dogs and/or amiodarone in cats. This is abstract-level; no drug doses are given here [3].
- End-tidal CO2 monitoring helps verify intubation, identify return of spontaneous circulation and evaluate CPR quality [4].
- After return of circulation, stabilization and neurological improvement may take 48 to 72 hours [5].
- RECOVER 2026 first aid: start bystander basic life support immediately for out-of-hospital arrest if risk to the rescuer is low (strong, moderate quality), transport to the nearest open veterinary clinic as soon as possible (strong, moderate quality), and continue BLS during transport with priority on rescuer safety (strong, expert opinion) [6].
