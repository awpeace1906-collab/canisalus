# Tension pneumothorax

Module id: `tension-pneumothorax`  |  Domain: trauma  |  Status: **draft**  |  Kind: clinical

> DRAFT for review. Nothing here is approved. Do not use for patient care.

## Why this matters

Tension pneumothorax kills quickly, and the human needle-decompression rules that responders know were built on human chest walls and fail often even there [4][5][6].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- Human needle-decompression rules (site, needle length) come from human chest walls and often fail there; do not carry them over to a dog [4][5][6].
- In dogs, iatrogenic pneumothorax (for example from a misplaced nasogastric tube) is a recognized cause [1][2].

## Recognition

- In dogs with traumatic pneumomediastinum, common signs were tachycardia, tachypnea, subcutaneous emphysema, pneumothorax and dyspnea [3].
- Pneumothorax followed nasogastric tube misplacement in the airway in 0.3% of 4,777 dogs in one study; 9 of 13 affected dogs developed respiratory compromise [1].
- Whether a dog's pneumothorax stays on one side is disputed: disease stayed in one pleural cavity unless the mediastinum was injured in a 39-dog experiment [8], but 5 of 6 dogs in a 2019 ultrasound series had pneumothorax on both sides [9]. Assess both sides. See the respiratory pathophysiology module.

## Management

- In the 13-dog series, 11 dogs needed thoracocentesis and 5 a thoracostomy tube; 5 arrested and 5 survived to discharge [1]. In a 6-dog series, pneumothorax resolved in all, with thoracocentesis in 3 and a chest tube in 1 [2].
- In 52 dogs and cats with traumatic pneumomediastinum, most also had pneumothorax needing thoracocentesis, and 26 of 28 dogs with known outcome survived [3].
- Human and animal-model context, not canine evidence: prehospital needle decompression was effective in only 18% in one adult series [6], failed in 58% of attempts in a swine model [5], and a human systematic review advised a needle longer than 7 cm [4]. A US EMS position statement summarizes the human evidence [7].
