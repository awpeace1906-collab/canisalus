# Hemorrhagic shock and the canine spleen

Module id: `hemorrhagic-shock-and-the-canine-spleen`  |  Domain: pathophysiology  |  Status: **draft**  |  Kind: pathophysiology

> DRAFT for review. Nothing here is approved. Do not use for patient care.

## Why this matters

A dog's contractile spleen changes how blood loss shows up, so a human clinician's read of early vital signs and blood counts can understate how much blood a dog has lost.

## Claims that differ from human practice

- The dog has a large, contractile spleen that can modify its response to bleeding, unlike the human spleen.
- A blood count that has not yet fallen does not rule out major blood loss in a dog.
- Stopping the bleeding comes first. Fluids are supportive, and blood products are preferred for severe hemorrhage where available.

## Dog vs human compare rows

- **Splenic reserve in hemorrhage** (human basis: cited): human: The human spleen does not act as a large blood reservoir under hemorrhage. | canine: The dog has a large, contractile spleen that can modify its cardiovascular response to bleeding. A letter citing other studies describes spleens of the pig, cat and dog (unlike the human) sequestering a large share of blood volume (up to 20 to 25%) and autotransfusing a substantial volume under severe hemorrhage. The figures come from a letter, not a primary study.
- **Blood count after hemorrhage** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Hemoglobin and hematocrit typically lag behind acute blood loss until fluid shifts or resuscitation dilute the blood. | canine: In conscious dogs with intact spleens bled 41% of blood volume, hemoglobin did not change; the authors attributed this to splenic contraction adding concentrated red cells, later diluted by extracellular fluid moving into the vessels.
- **Outcome without the spleen** (human basis: cited): human: Not applicable. | canine: All 10 splenectomized conscious dogs died after 41% blood volume hemorrhage in one experimental study, and splenectomized dogs showed greater loss of left ventricular performance in another.
- **Markers of shock** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Tachycardia, low blood pressure, raised lactate and base deficit. | canine: The physiologic response to whole blood loss in dogs is characterized clinically by increased heart rate, decreased systolic blood pressure and increased shock index, and biochemically by increased lactate and lower base excess.
- **Resuscitation strategy** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Hemorrhage control first, with blood products favoured over large-volume crystalloid in severe hemorrhage. | canine: A review supports prioritizing hemorrhage control with judicious crystalloid, then early consideration of platelets, plasma and red cells. Another proposes hemostatic resuscitation for dogs, using plasma and whole blood while minimizing crystalloids and colloids, and states more work is needed.
