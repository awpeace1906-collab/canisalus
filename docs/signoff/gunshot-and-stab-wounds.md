# Gunshot and stab wounds

## Why this matters

Gunshot wounds were the commonest external cause of death in deployed military working dogs, and in civilian dogs a projectile in the chest carried a much higher risk of death than wounds elsewhere [1][3].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- In 166 dogs with projectile injuries, the risk of a fatal outcome was 14.4 times higher with thoracic injuries; most gunshot injuries did not cause death, and the commonest projectile was an airgun pellet (62%) [3].
- Working dogs are at special risk: gunshot wounds and knife or sharp-object injuries were more common in deployed military working dogs than in civilian dogs, and survival to discharge was good in both groups [2].

## Recognition

- Among 92 military working dogs that died in deployment (Iraq and Afghanistan, 2001 to 2013), injuries caused 77.2% of deaths; the commonest external injuries were gunshot wounds (31.5%), explosion or blast (26.1%) and heat stress (9.8%) [1].
- In a registry comparison, penetrating injuries were the most frequent trauma type across the three groups; for deployed military working dogs specifically, blunt and combined blunt-and-penetrating trauma were equally common [2].
- In 37 civilian cats and dogs, working breeds were overrepresented (78%), limb wounds were the most numerous, and a higher animal trauma triage score predicted the need for surgery or blood products and a lower chance of survival to discharge [4].
- Gunshot fractures: in 97 animals with 137 fractures, 20 of 37 with follow-up had a poor outcome (death, major complication or amputation); extensive soft tissue injury at the fracture raised the odds of a poor outcome [5].

## Management

- RECOVER 2026 first aid for severe limb bleeding not controlled by direct pressure: apply a tourniquet to unconscious dogs (strong, very low quality) and suggest it for conscious dogs (weak, very low quality); not when risk to the rescuer is high [6].
- The authors of the gunshot fracture series conclude that avoiding a poor outcome likely requires early aggressive wound management [5].
