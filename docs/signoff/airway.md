## Why this matters

When a dog cannot be intubated and cannot be oxygenated, delay to an emergency front-of-neck airway has been linked to deaths in a small case series; airway skill and timing are the issue, not species-specific anatomy alone [1][2].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- In five small-animal cannot-intubate, cannot-oxygenate cases (3 dogs, 2 cats) all died, and emergency front-of-neck airway access was late, not attempted or not possible in several; the authors argue for early recognition and a defined trigger [1].
- Brachycephalic dogs in airway crisis who needed immediate intubation had 71% survival to discharge and a 48% complication rate; longer intubation time raised pneumonia risk [3].

## Recognition

- In healthy anesthetized dogs, final-year students took a median 54 s to intubate versus 12 s for qualified veterinarians; the authors recommend a 55 s supervisor take-over limit and pre-oxygenation to limit hypoxemia [2]. This was an anesthesia setting, not a field emergency.
- Brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome in crisis can require immediate intubation; French Bulldogs regurgitated more often than other brachycephalic breeds [3].

## Management

- RECOVER 2026: give oxygen to dogs and cats with difficulty breathing or hypoxemia during transport (strong, expert opinion); when monitoring is available, titrate to an SpO2 of 95 to 97% (strong, moderate quality) [4].
- Choking, conscious dog or cat, low risk to the rescuer: up to 5 back blows over the caudodorsal thorax, lateral to the spine, then alternative techniques if unsuccessful (strong, very low quality). Unconscious with suspected airway foreign body: no blind finger sweeps (strong, expert opinion); start chest compressions per the out-of-hospital CPR algorithm (strong, very low quality) [4].
- Time-limited attempts and early escalation are the lesson from the CICO case series; the authors propose a guideline for emergency front-of-neck access [1].
