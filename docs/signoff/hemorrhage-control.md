# Hemorrhage control

## Why this matters

Hemorrhage is a leading cause of preventable death in trauma, and blood loss from a major artery can kill a dog in as little as 3 to 5 minutes [1].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- A tourniquet is not the default first step in dogs: direct pressure and packing come first [1][2].
- A too-tight bandage around the neck, chest or abdomen can suffocate the dog [1].

## Recognition

- Most bleeding wounds are not initially life-threatening but become so with delay in control [1].
- Common sources of massive hemorrhage: a major artery or large vessel (for example the external jugular) or a highly vascular organ such as the liver or spleen [1].

## Management

- Direct pressure remains the most effective medical intervention for initial control [1].
- Methods for the exsanguinating dog: direct pressure, wound packing, hemostatic dressings, pressure bandage, and possibly a tourniquet [1][2].
- A tourniquet is lifesaving in people but has not been shown to be necessary for dogs with extremity injuries [1][2].
- A circumferential bandage that is too tight around the neck, thorax or abdomen can occlude the airway or restrict breathing. Non-circumferential bandages often do not stop arterial bleeding [1].
- When feasible, elevate and splint a bleeding extremity as an adjunct to other methods [1].
- Definitive control of massive internal hemorrhage is best achieved by early surgery [1].
- In one dog, junctional hemorrhage that failed pressure and packing was temporarily controlled with a Foley balloon catheter (single case report) [4].
- RECOVER 2026: apply a tourniquet to unconscious dogs and cats with severe extremity hemorrhage not controlled by direct pressure (strong, very low quality); suggested for conscious animals (weak, very low quality); recommended against when risk to the rescuer is high (strong, expert opinion) [5].
- The guideline flags that dog and cat limbs are more conical than human limbs, which may affect placement or slipping, and that commercial tourniquets may not fit small animals; there is no species-specific evidence [5].
