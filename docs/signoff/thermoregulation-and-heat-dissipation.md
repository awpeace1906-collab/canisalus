# Thermoregulation and heat dissipation

## Why this matters

Dogs lose heat mainly by panting, not sweating, so heat illness in a working dog develops and responds differently from heat illness in a person.

## Claims that differ from human practice

- Dogs lose heat mainly by panting and have fewer sweat glands than people, so anything that impairs panting removes their main way to cool.
- Cool first, transport second: a dog's temperature can keep rising after exercise stops.
- In the studies retrieved, partial water immersion cooled dogs faster than passive cooling or a cooling mat, and faster than alcohol on the paw pads.

## Dog vs human compare rows

- **Main route of heat loss** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Sweating: evaporation from the skin. | canine: Panting: evaporation from the upper respiratory tract, with air moved over the nasal and oral surfaces. Dogs have a reduced number of sweat glands compared with humans.
- **Where the water for evaporation comes from** (human basis: cited): human: Sweat glands over the skin. | canine: The lateral nasal glands supply much of the water for panting, and their function is described as analogous to sweat glands in man.
- **How heat stroke is defined** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Core temperature above about 40 °C with central nervous system dysfunction. | canine: Core temperature above 41 °C with central nervous system dysfunction, in one review.
- **Field cooling** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Cold-water immersion is a recognised first-line field treatment for exertional heat stroke. | canine: Partial water immersion cooled dogs faster than passive cooling or a cooling mat in one study (time to 39 °C: 16, 36 and 48 minutes). Cold water (0.1 to 15 °C) cooled dogs effectively in a canicross study with no hypothermia or adverse effects seen. Alcohol on the paw pads cooled less efficiently than immersion and raised heart rate more.
- **Temperature after exercise stops** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Core temperature generally falls once exertion stops and cooling begins. | canine: In a canicross study, a dog's temperature rose during the first 5 minutes after exercise in 24.4% of cooling profiles, and dogs housed in vehicles cooled less.
