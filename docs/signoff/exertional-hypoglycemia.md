## Why this matters

Low blood sugar can cause collapse, seizures and death in dogs, and the first-aid step differs by whether the dog is awake or seizing; the 2026 RECOVER guideline covers hypoglycemia in dogs generally, and no source specific to exertional hypoglycemia has been found [1].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- For a dog with symptomatic low blood sugar and no seizure, the guideline suggests a viscous simple carbohydrate such as corn syrup applied to the cheek, not swallowed liquid, provided this does not delay veterinary care (weak, very low quality) [1].
- Never put anything in the mouth of a seizing dog: the guideline recommends against oral or mucosal sugar during seizures because of bite risk to the rescuer (strong, expert opinion) [1].

## Recognition

- The guideline's examples of symptomatic hypoglycemia are lethargy, ataxia, collapse, abnormal mentation and vomiting, in animals such as those on insulin or oral hypoglycemic drugs [1].
- Hypoglycemia can also be found on a continuous glucose monitor or glucometer in an animal that has no signs [1].

## Management

- Asymptomatic dog with documented low glucose: feed dietary carbohydrate such as pet food (strong, very low quality); go to a veterinary hospital if signs appear or glucose has not at least normalized within 20 minutes of feeding [1].
- Symptomatic dog, not seizing: apply a viscous simple carbohydrate (for example corn syrup or glucose syrup) to the buccal mucosa between the upper premolars and the cheek, only if it does not delay veterinary care (weak, very low quality) [1].
- Seizing dog: nothing by mouth or on the mucosa (strong, expert opinion). Nothing by mouth either if there is perceived risk to the caregiver (strong, expert opinion) [1].
- The guideline notes that oral or buccal sugar may not resolve life-threatening hypoglycemia and should not delay transport for intravenous dextrose [1].
