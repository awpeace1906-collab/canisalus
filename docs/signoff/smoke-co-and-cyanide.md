# Smoke, CO, and cyanide

## Why this matters

Carbon monoxide and cyanide are the two systemic poisons in smoke, and a dog can look pink with a normal-looking saturation while carrying a dangerous carboxyhemoglobin level [1][3].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- Pulse oximetry can be falsely normal in carbon monoxide poisoning [1].
- In a canine model, cyanide rather than carbon monoxide caused the severe cardiovascular depression [4]. Human cyanide antidote practice has no retrieved canine evidence [5].

## Recognition

- Carbon monoxide poisoning after house fires was confirmed by CO-oximetry in 3 dogs with markedly raised carboxyhemoglobin [3].
- In 22 small animals after house fires, pink mucous membranes and moderate carboxyhemoglobinemia were found [2].

## Management

- RECOVER 2026 first aid: oxygen for dogs and cats with difficulty breathing or hypoxemia during transport (strong, expert opinion), titrated to an SpO2 of 95 to 97% when monitoring is available (strong, moderate quality). Note that pulse oximetry can read falsely normal in carbon monoxide exposure [7].
- High-flow nasal cannula oxygen eliminated carboxyhemoglobin in three dogs, with calculated half-lives of 79, 86 and 77 minutes, and all survived to discharge without delayed complications (case series of 3) [3].
- In a canine experimental model, carbon monoxide alone (peak carboxyhemoglobin 46%) had little effect on hemodynamic or metabolic variables, while an intravenous cyanide infusion cut cardiac output roughly in half and lowered oxygen consumption; these recovered within 15 minutes of stopping the infusion, except lactic acidosis [4].
- Human data, context only: cyanide poisoning is thought overlooked in fire victims, and hydroxocobalamin is the recommended antidote in humans [5]. Hyperbaric oxygen is not supported outside trials for carbon monoxide neurological sequelae in humans [6].
