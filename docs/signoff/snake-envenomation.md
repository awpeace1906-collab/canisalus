# Snake envenomation

## Why this matters

The syndrome depends on the snake and the region, from coagulopathy to paralysis to both, and most dogs in the series retrieved survived with antivenom and hospital care [1][2].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- In Australian eastern brown snake bites, dogs show mainly neurotoxicity plus coagulopathy, whereas in humans coagulopathy is of primary significance [3].
- The syndrome, and therefore the plan, depends on the snake: pit viper, brown snake and red-bellied black snake bites look different [1][2][4].
- In a large pit viper series, older age, lower body weight and thoracic bites carried lower survival [1].

## Recognition

- Eastern brown snake in dogs: lower motor neuropathy in 66% and preparalytic collapse in 31% on presentation, venom-induced consumptive coagulopathy in 92% and hemorrhage in 30% [2].
- Red-bellied black snake in 17 dogs: hemolysis, raised CK, pigmenturia and a mildly prolonged clotting time, with bite-site swelling in 53% [4].
- Pit viper (crotalid) envenomation in 282 dogs: 96.1% survived to discharge [1].

## Management

- Three commercial crotalid antivenoms had similar survival. Infusion reactions were more frequent with the IgG product than with either F(ab')2 product [1].
- In eastern brown snake envenomation, antivenom overall was associated with a survival advantage, but a higher total did not add benefit. Median time to normal coagulation tests was 24 hours [2].
- Some severely paralysed dogs needed mechanical ventilation [3][4].
- Human data: evidence that antivenom corrects venom-induced consumption coagulopathy is limited and varies by snake, and fresh frozen plasma may speed recovery in bleeding patients [6].
