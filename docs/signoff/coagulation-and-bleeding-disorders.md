# Coagulation and bleeding disorders

## Why this matters

Clot-consuming snake venoms acted faster on dog plasma than on human plasma in the laboratory, and breed-linked bleeding disorders such as von Willebrand disease change how much a dog bleeds from an ordinary injury.

## Claims that differ from human practice

- In vitro, all 11 procoagulant snake venoms tested acted faster on dog plasma than on cat or human plasma, so dogs may become coagulopathic sooner after envenomation; dog plasma also clotted faster on its own than cat plasma.
- Von Willebrand disease was found in 1.43% of 350 dogs of various breeds in one Brazilian survey, and a case report notes Doberman Pinschers have a high prevalence; a history of bleeding, or a predisposed breed, matters before procedures.

## Dog vs human compare rows

- **Speed of clotting and vulnerability to procoagulant venom** (human basis: cited): human: Procoagulant venoms acted more slowly on human plasma than on dog plasma in the same test system. | canine: All 11 venoms tested, including eastern brown snake venom, acted faster on dog plasma than on cat or human plasma. Dog plasma also clotted faster spontaneously after recalcification than cat plasma (no human comparison was reported). In Australia, only 31% of dogs survived eastern brown snake bites without antivenom against 66% of cats.
- **Inherited bleeding disorder: von Willebrand disease** (human basis: general-medical-knowledge): human: Von Willebrand disease is the commonest inherited bleeding disorder in people. | canine: In 350 dogs in Sao Paulo State, Brazil, with no history of abnormal bleeding, 1.43% had von Willebrand disease; von Willebrand factor antigen was the best diagnostic test. A case report states Doberman Pinschers have a high prevalence, and a Pembroke Welsh Corgi with the disease had unexpected bleeding and a late procedural complication.
