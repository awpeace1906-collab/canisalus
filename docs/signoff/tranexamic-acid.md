# Tranexamic acid

> DRAFT for review. Nothing here is approved. Do not use for patient care.

## Why this matters

Tranexamic acid helps trauma patients if given early, but no canine trauma trial exists, and dog blood appears to need a different amount than human blood to block clot breakdown; human doses must not be copied [1].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- In human trauma, tranexamic acid lowered death when given within 3 hours of injury and raised it when given later; in dogs there is no equivalent trial [1].
- In viscoelastic testing, the dose needed to inhibit fibrinolysis was about 10 times higher in dog blood than in human blood, and it is unclear how this relates to appropriate dosing in dogs [1].

## Recognition

- Tranexamic acid blocks clot breakdown (plasmin) without causing clot formation, and was linked to better survival in large human trauma studies, strongest when given early [1].
- Human data also show a possible rise in venous thromboembolism after tranexamic acid, which remains unresolved [1].

## Management

- Dog evidence: tranexamic acid inhibits fibrinolysis in dogs in laboratory studies; in 55 dogs given it for intraoperative or postoperative bleeding, treated dogs had higher blood pressure, lower shock index and were less often in shock at 24 hours, though they received more plasma; in another series transfusion volumes did not differ [1].
- Epsilon-aminocaproic acid, a related drug: greyhounds undergoing amputation that did not receive it were 5.7 times more likely to bleed, and a 122-dog series found it well tolerated [1].
