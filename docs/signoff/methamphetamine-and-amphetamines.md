# Methamphetamine and amphetamines

## Why this matters

Stimulant exposure is a duty hazard for working dogs. Street drugs are often adulterated, so presentations are non-specific, and most dogs improve with prompt treatment but severe cases can die [1][4].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- Presentations are non-specific because illicit drugs are adulterated with other substances [1].
- Rapid on-site drug tests give many false positives because of low specificity [1].

## Recognition

- A methamphetamine case report (one 3.7 kg dog): extreme agitation, seizures, tachycardia, hyperthermia, hypertension, dilated pupils and disseminated intravascular coagulation [3].
- In a lisdexamfetamine case, early neurological signs progressed to severe respiratory and cardiovascular signs and cardiopulmonary arrest, with widespread hemorrhage at necropsy [4].
- One dog with amphetamine toxicosis developed severe rhabdomyolysis with myoglobinuria and recovered without kidney injury [5].

## Management

- With prompt treatment the prognosis of amphetamine toxicosis in dogs is generally considered good, but a fatal case shows that prompt treatment does not always prevent death [4].
- Intravenous lipid emulsion in 313 poisoned dogs (mostly mixed or unidentified toxicants, not stimulants specifically) had a positive effect in 74%, worsened signs in 4% and had suspected adverse effects in 6%; overall survival was 96%. The authors advise careful individual selection [6].
- In one amphetamine case, serum amphetamine concentrations were lower 1 hour after lipid emulsion, which was the only treatment given in that hour (single case) [2].
