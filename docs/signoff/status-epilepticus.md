## Why this matters

Seizure emergencies are unlikely to stop on their own and become harder to treat the longer they last, with high morbidity and mortality [1][5].

## Claims that differ from human practice

- Intranasal benzodiazepine by atomizer has been studied in dogs as a first-line option when IV access is not available [2][3].
- Treat early: prolonged seizure activity becomes progressively more resistant to treatment [5].

## Recognition

- Status epilepticus is broadly defined as seizures lasting more than 5 minutes, or multiple seizures without full recovery between them. Untreated, it can cause permanent brain damage and systemic complications (cardiorespiratory collapse, shock, acidosis), and complication risk rises with seizure duration [4].
- Overall mortality among dogs presenting with status epilepticus has been reported at 25.3 to 38.5%; about 0.5 to 2.6% of dogs admitted to emergency hospitals have it [4].

## Management

- Benzodiazepines are the first-line drugs for canine status epilepticus and cluster seizures [1][4].
- An ACVIM consensus statement recommends an early, rapid, stage-based approach, with management of complications and underlying causes alongside seizure medication [1].
- In a randomized trial in dogs in status epilepticus before IV access, intranasal midazolam by atomizer stopped seizures in 14 of 20 dogs versus 3 of 15 with rectal diazepam [2].
- Intranasal and intravenous midazolam had similar success (76% and 61%), and intranasal was faster when the time to place an IV catheter was counted [3].
- In the 2021 review's assessment, intranasal midazolam is likely an effective and safe first choice at home and in hospital, especially when IV access has not been established; rectal diazepam is unlikely to be as effective [4].
- Rectal diazepam stopped status epilepticus in about 20% of dogs versus about 70% with intranasal midazolam in one open-label trial [4].
- When time to place an IV catheter was counted, the IV route was slower: median seizure cessation 1 minute (IV) versus 0.5 minutes (intranasal), with 4.5 minutes to establish IV access versus 1.6 minutes to give the intranasal dose [4].
- Intramuscular, buccal and sublingual benzodiazepines have no clinical trials in dogs, only pharmacokinetic data; the review calls their evidence insufficient and notes bite risk to owners. Diazepam is not advised intramuscularly because of erratic absorption [4].
- In two canine trials, intranasal midazolam worked across breeds including brachycephalic dogs; difficulty applying the atomizer occurred in under 24% of dogs and was linked to unfamiliar staff. No intranasal device designed for dogs exists; trials used a human mucosal atomization device [4].
- Only diazepam is licensed for small animals; midazolam use is off-label [4].
- Sedation and ataxia were common after benzodiazepine treatment [2][3].
- In an owner survey, intranasal midazolam was perceived as more effective than rectal diazepam. This is owner perception, not a trial [5].
