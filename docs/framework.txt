CaniSalus — Framework for Reviewer Sign-off
September 28, 2026 · Andrew Peace
Purpose and how to review
CaniSalus (“Salus” for short) gives human-trained clinicians (paramedics, flight/CCT crews, ED physicians) canine-specific emergency guidance for working dogs in duress, until the dog reaches a veterinarian. It is a bridge to veterinary care, never a replacement for it.
Audiences: K9 handlers (Tier 0), prehospital ALS, flight/CCT, human ED staff, and veterinary teams using it as a shared reference with human partners.
Reviewers are asked to sign off on three things:
The environment ladder and what each tier may reasonably attempt.
The content domains and module list: what is missing, what should be cut.
The safety guardrails, especially dosing rules.
This document is a framework. It contains no drug doses. Every clinical value will be sourced from veterinary references and signed off per module before release.
Please comment directly on any line. Veterinary reviewers: flag anything a human clinician would get dangerously wrong. Physician reviewers: flag anything that assumes veterinary background a human clinician lacks.

About CaniSalus
Pronounced kay-nih-SAY-lus. Most people just say “Salus.”
The name: canis is Latin for dog. Salus is the Latin word for health, and also for safety and rescue. CaniSalus means the dog’s health and rescue: the work that happens between the moment a working dog goes down and the moment a veterinarian takes over.
Why CaniSalus exists: working dogs get hurt where people get hurt: on scene, in the field, in the back of an ambulance. The first clinician to reach them is usually trained to care for humans. The canine knowledge that clinician needs is scattered across tactical courses, state protocols, and references written for veterinarians. CaniSalus puts it in one place, organized around the setting you are actually in, with one goal: get the dog to a veterinarian in the best shape possible.
CaniSalus is a bridge to veterinary care, never a replacement for it. Every module is reviewed by both a veterinarian and a physician before release.

Why the order holds: a human ED has more resuscitation capacity than most general-practice vet offices. A GP vet is still closer to definitive care, because it has canine blood, species-specific surgery, and legal authority. Human ED sits in the middle, and it is the default because the primary audience is human clinicians, where the cross-training gap is widest.
Why flags, not rank: the table values are defaults, not facts about every site. A rural vet may be closed overnight; a trauma center may have a vet partnership. Modules read the capability flags, so users can adjust their setting (for example, “my vet clinic has a ventilator”) without the app hardcoding exceptions.

Header: title, category, reversibility tag, last-verified date, reviewer sign-offs.
Why this matters: one or two sentences on why a working dog dies of this.
What changes from human: the one to three things a human-trained clinician will instinctively get wrong. Each item becomes a course learning objective.
Content: recognition, management, and procedures, with visual toggles.
Environment lens: for the user’s tier, what you can do here, what to leave for the next level, and the transfer trigger.

Safety guardrails
The most important rule: the app never shows a human dose in place of a canine dose. If no canine-sourced dose exists, the app says so and directs the user to a veterinarian or poison control.
Canine source required. Every drug entry needs a veterinary citation for species, dose, route, and maximum. Builds fail if one is missing.
Weight is mandatory. Doses are not calculated without a weight. An estimated weight is labeled as estimated on every result screen.
Human-product warnings. Human formulations with canine hazards (xylitol-containing liquids and ODTs, combination products with acetaminophen or NSAIDs) carry a warning wherever they appear.
MDR1 flag. If the K9 profile marks MDR1-positive or unknown in a herding breed, affected drugs show a warning.
Dual sign-off. A module ships only when both a veterinary reviewer (ideally DACVECC) and a physician reviewer have signed off. Unsigned modules are hidden, not shown as drafts.
Staleness. Each module carries a last-verified date. Modules past their review interval show a banner, reusing Kairos tooling.
Scope reminder. Every module links to the jurisdiction card. The app states on launch that it bridges to veterinary care.
K9 profile card: handlers can store weight, breed, DEA 1 blood type, MDR1 status, current meds, baseline labs, and vet contact ahead of time. The profile feeds the dose engine and the handoff generator.
