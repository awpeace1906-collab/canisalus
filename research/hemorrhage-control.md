## What the sources support

- **Priority (B1).** Trauma hemorrhage must be addressed immediately; most bleeding wounds are not initially life-threatening but become so with delay. Loss from a major arterial source (femoral or carotid) may cause death in as little as 3-5 minutes.
- **Methods for the exsanguinating small animal (B1, B2).** Direct pressure, wound packing, hemostatic dressings, pressure bandage, and possibly a tourniquet.
- **Tourniquet in dogs (B1, B2).** Both state that a tourniquet is a lifesaving intervention in people but has not been shown to be necessary, and is not considered a necessary immediate-action life-saving intervention, for dogs with extremity injuries. *This supports the framework's "packing and pressure first" but stops short of "never".* B1 says "possibly."
- **Direct pressure (B1).** Direct pressure is the most effective "medical" intervention for initial control. Elevate and splint a bleeding extremity as an adjunct.
- **Bandage hazards (B1).** A circumferential compressive bandage that is too tight around the neck, thorax or abdomen can occlude the airway, restrict chest expansion or prevent respiration. Non-circumferential bandages often do not generate enough pressure to stop arterial hemorrhage, particularly after fluid resuscitation restores systolic pressure.
- **Non-compressible hemorrhage (B1).** Definitive hemostasis for massive internal hemorrhage is best achieved by early surgery.
- **Junctional hemorrhage (B4).** In one dog with a transected femoral artery and vein, pressure and gauze packing failed and a Foley balloon catheter (10 Fr, inflated with 7.5 mL saline) controlled bleeding as a temporary measure before surgery. *Single case report. Not a recommendation.*
- **Human tourniquet evidence, for context only.** B5: tourniquet time over 4 hours was associated with lower limb salvage (57.1%) and higher mortality (7.1%) than under 2 hours; application under 2 hours was comparatively safe. B6: nerve palsy was the most common complication with prolonged use. **Neither is canine data.**
- **TXA and battlefield adjuncts (B3).** B3 discusses tourniquets, hemostatic dressings, antifibrinolytics such as tranexamic acid, REBOA and hemostatic foams as candidates for translation. The abstract does not say which are validated in dogs.

## What changes from human (candidates)

1. The tourniquet is not the default first move in dogs (B1, B2); packing and pressure come first. *Confirm with the K9TCCC text, which was not retrievable.*
2. A tight circumferential bandage on the neck, chest or abdomen can suffocate the dog (B1).

## Edwards 2020 Part 1, full text read (added 2026-09-30)

Frontiers Vet Sci 7:571368 (PMID 33521075, open access PMC7841008). Read in full. Findings now in the module: direct pressure technique, hemostatic dressings (one 10-dog chitosan study, 12 of 14 femoral arteries), windlass tourniquets ineffective in dogs (tapered limb), elastic wrap used as pressure bandage, junctional tourniquets not evaluated, XSTAT and iTClamp not studied in dogs, REBOA cadaver study (15 of 15, 10 to 48 kg).
Tranexamic acid (new module `tranexamic-acid`): the review reports ~10-fold higher amount needed to inhibit fibrinolysis in dog vs human blood in vitro (viscoelastic testing). **No dose is entered**; the reviewer must choose a canine source. Also recorded: EACA in greyhounds (5.7x more likely to bleed without), 122-dog EACA series, 55-dog TXA series.
Agreement with RECOVER 2026: both say tourniquets are for bleeding not controlled by direct pressure and flag the conical limb. Emphasis differs (Edwards: rarely needed).
