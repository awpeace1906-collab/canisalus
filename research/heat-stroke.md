## What the sources support (candidate statements)

Each line is only as strong as its cited abstract. Reviewers: confirm, correct or strike.

- **Cool first, transport second.** H4 and H6 both describe this message. H6 cites 2016 guidance on prehospital management of dogs with heat-related illness advising "cool first, transport second" with cold-water immersion and evaporative cooling (water with air movement) as the optimal approaches. *The 2016 guidance itself was not retrieved.*
- **Temperature can keep rising after exercise stops.** H4: in 28/115 profiles (24.4%) the dog's temperature increased in the first 5 minutes post-exercise; not being housed in a vehicle was associated with more cooling.
- **Water immersion cools faster than passive cooling or a cooling mat.** H3: time to 39 °C GI temperature was 16 min (immersion) vs 36 min (cooling mat) vs 48 min (control); immersion was effective even when the water was as warm as ambient (30 °C). H1: partial water immersion cooled more efficiently than isopropyl alcohol on paw pads, which also raised heart rate more.
- **Cold water is safe in the study populations.** H4: cold-water immersion (0.1-15.0 °C) cooled dogs effectively; no dog became hypothermic and no adverse effects were seen with any method.
- **When immersion is not possible.** H2: in dogs with normal mental status and able to pause panting, a trained voluntary head dunk with limited water ingestion produced the lowest mean core temperature in the first 5 minutes and prevented the initial post-exercise rise. H5: tap water or alcohol on glabrous skin plus a fan cooled effectively from early on; ingesting ice water was the only single method that lowered core temperature without a fan.
- **Risk factors and presentation (H7).** Core temperature > 41 °C with CNS dysfunction; risk factors include heat and humidity, exertion, obesity, body weight > 15 kg, Labrador retrievers, brachycephalic breeds, upper airway obstruction, prolonged seizures. Common signs: acute collapse, tachypnea, spontaneous bleeding, shock signs, mental abnormalities. Mortality around 50% despite early cooling and intensive care. Complications: rhabdomyolysis, AKI, ARDS, sepsis, DIC.

## What changes from human (candidates)

1. Cooling method: evidence here favors immersion or wet-plus-airflow for dogs, with alcohol on paw pads inferior to immersion (H1). Reviewers: does this contradict any human EHS habit the audience holds, such as ice packs at the neck or axillae? H2 tested neck ice packs and wet neck/axilla towels, and dunking was best.
2. Dogs cool by panting, not sweating: H7 notes functional differences between dogs and humans. Reviewers to state the practical consequence for a human clinician.

## RECOVER 2026 first-aid guideline (heat stroke, section 3.3) read in full text (added 2026-09-30)

Thawley 2026 (PMID 42640821). Answers the open question of when to stop cooling: cool to a rectal temperature of 39.7 to 40 C then stop (weak, expert opinion); with stertor or stridor continue until panting improves or 38.6 C. Cool running water on the trunk, especially the belly. The framework's 39.4 C figure is not what the guideline says. Evidence for prehospital cooling is very low quality; the guideline calls for a multicenter study. Reviewer still decides (TODO decision 10).
